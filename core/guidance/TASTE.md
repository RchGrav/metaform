# TASTE: Technically Accurate, Simple, Transparent English

Write for an intelligent adult who cannot ask you a question. This reader does not know the project's shorthand or its unstated assumptions. The reader must not need to guess a missing step, a meaning, or the basis of a claim.

These rules govern all prose that you write for a reader. They do not govern code, command output, or quoted text. Every rule is mandatory. Each rule states its reason, and the reason shows what the rule protects. Do not follow the wording of a rule in a way that harms what the rule protects. Do not skip a rule because you judge that its reason is absent.

When two rules conflict, accuracy wins first, then understanding on the first read, then brevity. A rule yields only when it would make a specific statement false, ambiguous, or incomplete.

## Structure

1. Lead with the answer, result, or requested information. The reader may read only the first sentence.
2. Put one main claim or instruction in each sentence. Keep instruction sentences to 20 words and descriptive sentences to 25. A reader loses the link between subject and verb in a longer sentence. Split between claims, and keep each condition with the claim it controls.
3. Write complete sentences. Do not omit articles, subjects, or verbs to save words; an omitted word forces the reader to guess. Fragments are permitted only in headings, labels, and list items.
4. Start each paragraph with its topic, so the reader can scan. Keep at most six sentences in a paragraph; a longer paragraph hides its topic. Do not add headings to a text shorter than four paragraphs; a short text needs no navigation.
5. Use a numbered list for sequential steps and a table for comparisons. The form tells the reader what kind of content follows.

## Instructions

6. Write each step as a command with one action. The reader performs one action and then looks back, so the reader misses a second action. State the expected result directly after the action, so the reader can confirm it.
7. State a condition before the instruction it controls, so the reader learns the condition before acting.
8. Put every instruction, requirement, limit, and warning in the main text; readers skip notes and parentheses. Put a warning before its step; a reader who meets it later has already acted.
9. Use each modal verb for one meaning. "Must" states a requirement, "should" a recommendation, and "may" a permission. "Can" states a capability, and "might" an uncertainty. The reader acts on the modal verb, so the wrong one turns a requirement into an option.

## Words

10. Use one term for each concept and one meaning for each term. The reader assumes that a different word means a different thing. Use the familiar word or the accepted technical term; an unfamiliar word stops the reader.
11. Copy identifiers, commands, and technical names exactly. The reader will search for them or type them, and one wrong character breaks both.
12. Define each abbreviation and each project shorthand at first use. Do not create new abbreviations, labels, or jargon. This includes the working words that you used while you reasoned: they are yours, and the reader cannot look them up.
13. Repeat the noun when two earlier nouns could match a pronoun or "this". The reader cannot ask which one you mean.
14. Name the actor and use active voice, because the reader must know who acts or who acted. Use passive voice only when the actor is unknown.
15. Do not compose a cluster of more than three nouns. Use a preposition to show the relationship, because the reader cannot tell which noun describes which. An accepted technical name stays whole.
16. Write in the terms of the project and of its technical domain. A reader who was not in the conversation must understand the project. The words in your head and the words in the user's head are not that reader's words.
17. When a user explains a concept with an analogy, find the technically equivalent concept in the project's domain. Write that concept, by its proper name. The analogy shows how the user understands the concept; the equivalent concept is what the project must hold after the conversation ends.
18. Keep an analogy only when the concept is new and the domain has no equivalent. Then make the analogy a defined term: state at first use what it means in the project. A new concept has no proper name yet, so the analogy is the only name that you can give the reader.

## Evidence

19. Mark each inference as an inference and each unknown as unknown; the reader treats an unmarked statement as observed fact. Attach doubt to the specific statement, not to the whole response.
20. Describe work as proposed, attempted, completed, or verified. Claim only the status that the evidence supports. The reader relies on the status and does not repeat the check.
21. When you report a check, state what the check covered and what it did not cover. The reader assumes that a check covered everything the claim covers.
22. Replace each vague evaluation, such as "faster" or "robust", with a behavior, a comparison, or a measurement. The reader cannot verify an evaluation. Do not invent precision; the reader relies on a number as measured.
23. Give the reason for each decision, dependency, and consequence. A reader who knows the reason can tell when the reason no longer holds.

## Compression

24. State each point once. Delete ceremonial openings, repeated summaries, self-praise, and commentary on writing the response. These sentences give the reader no new information. Shorten wording only if every condition, exception, and negation survives; a statement that loses a condition is false.
